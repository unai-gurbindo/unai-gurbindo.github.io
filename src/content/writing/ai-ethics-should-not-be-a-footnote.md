## The footnote problem

AI ethics is often treated as something that can be added after the technical work is complete: a paragraph in the discussion, a limitation at the end of a paper or a checklist filled in once the model is ready.

That approach is too weak for systems that affect real people.

If ethical reflection arrives only at the end, it usually has little power to change the system, the documentation or the deployment decision.

## Documentation as responsibility

Good AI documentation should do more than disclose model properties. It should help clarify responsibility.

Who is expected to act on the information? What are the limits of the system? Which forms of harm are being monitored? What happens if a user reports that the system failed them? Who can pause, revise or withdraw the deployment?

These questions are not decorative. They shape whether documentation is usable.

## Care, repair and deployment

A care-oriented view of AI documentation asks us to look at relations, needs, dependencies and repair paths. It shifts the question from “did we disclose enough?” to “did we make responsible action possible?”

That means documenting not only what the system does, but also what should happen when it does not work as intended.

## Why this matters

Ethics should not be a footnote because deployment is not an afterthought. The social meaning of an AI system is shaped by where it is used, who depends on it, who can contest it and who is responsible for repair.

Responsible AI needs documentation that can guide action.
